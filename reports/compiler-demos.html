Existing tools and methods

Stanza supplies the relevant grammatical links, and AMR represents modifiers, shared nodes and conjunction/disjunction. Our cached examples already contain that information. AMR quantification is shallow; a correct graph does not remove the need for defined logical scope. A pretrained Stanza coreference module exists but was not benchmarked here.

Logic-LM already uses symbolic translation and solver-error refinement. PICARD constrains formal syntax during decoding. These support trying the proposed alternatives; neither guarantees faithful compilation of these media claims.

      General changes tested
      • Remove duplicate auxiliary frames. When the parser supplies both “are” and “covered,” keep the lexical frame and retain the tense information. Genuine copulas such as “is gray” remain. This alone raises direct automatic passes to 1,960.
      • Keep attached adverbs. Previously omitted words such as “back,” “in” and “below” become conditions attached to their actual event or noun. They do not receive word-ignore credit. Scope-sensitive words such as “likely,” “presumably,” “only” and “all” are excluded from this repair. The final condition reaches 1,978 direct passes.
      • Resolve explicit interval references. “During this time” can inherit a unique stated parent interval on the same compiled event. A missing event still causes refusal. An omitted end date is resolved only within a same-day range; no midnight rollover is guessed. Whole-phrase SRL arguments are replaced when a phrase relation supplies their explicit object.
      • Test nominal events separately. “The collapse of a bridge blocks a road” can use one event variable for collapse and the subject of blocks. But lexical typing mistakes camera shots and electoral ridings for actions. Nominal typing stays off by default; these diagnostics are excluded from the corpus gains.
      These are conventional parsing repairs. NomBank explicitly annotates nominal predicates and their arguments; our WordNet/context heuristic is not a trained nominal semantic-role parser or a general word-sense disambiguator.

        What changed, and why it is general
        • One value normalizer across argument positions: calendar dates, date–time pairs, durations and supported measurements use the same handling. Repeated time arguments keep their types. “Flight 2025” stays an identifier; a video time code is not silently converted into a clock.
        • Phrase-level relations: supported motion phrases such as “relative to” and “with respect to” bind a reference object. Supported spatial phrases bind a location. The governing predicate and dependency links constrain the interpretation. This is a bounded phrase inventory, not a complete preposition disambiguator.
        • Source alignments: each normalized value/relation records the original character span that produced it. A span receives credit only if its typed value or bound relation survives compilation. This supplements the word check; it does not prove correct meaning.
        • Semantic checks: an explicit time buried in an opaque phrase, or “During this time” without an interval reference, requests review. Copied quotations and typed raw times are handled separately.

        Why simpler sentences still fail
        • The compiler can reject simple, meaningful sentences. “Two trucks drive along a dock towards the ship” gives two direction arguments. The current conversion permits only one distinct entity-valued argument per role, so repeated ARGM-DIR is rejected. Rewording may change the parser output, but the underlying limitation is the compiler interface.
        • Splitting does not add missing logical meaning. “The video has no audio” requires absence over possible audio events. A positive audio entity followed by a negated event is not equivalent. A speed such as 20–25 km/h also needs a value, range and unit, rather than a count of objects.
        • The subclaims must still share participants and context. “Robot 1” and “Robot 2” have the same noun head; our conservative aligner currently refuses that ambiguity instead of using the explicit IDs. The second-shot vehicle example has useful short sentences, but the adapter requires a simple source sentence to recover the depicted context.
        • The model sometimes changes the claim. It turns no narration into positive and negative narration; building housing a publisher into a building being constructed; or a sign quotation into world assertions. Successful JSON output and short clauses do not establish faithfulness.
        What the 31-case experiment actually found
        Of 29 unrecovered cases, 12 stop at component compilation, 10 at entity alignment, 3 at source-context alignment and 2 at conflicting descriptions. Two additional generated programs are rejected on review. These are pipeline stages, not independent root causes: an unfaithful split may already fail an earlier check.
        The model cannot solve missing compiler semantics just by simplifying syntax. Conversely, our conservative alignment can reject useful simplifications. The current results therefore mix model errors, compiler limitations and overly strict matching; they do not prove that all 449 claims are intrinsically difficult.

          When to stop compiler optimization
          My recommendation is to freeze the current grammar rules as the comparison baseline now. Keep correcting basic source-alignment, quotation-protection and validation bugs, but stop adding an open-ended rule for each new sentence shape.
