1. Paper direction and scope

Chosen paper direction

Compile a claim into a probabilistic logic program, connect its predicates to visual, audio, speech, and on-screen-text evidence, and infer whether the claim is supported. Binding functions can use predefined rules or learned conditional distributions. The inference and learning backend may be DeepProbLog or a comparable existing system.

The paper is a methods paper about a common representation and learning procedure for multimodal claim verification. When claims come from model-generated text, the same verifier can identify unsupported or contradicted claims. The paper direction is accepted; the implementation and empirical claims still require validation.

Binding means whether the participants, attributes, actions, and sources are related as the claim says—for example, who did what to whom. Recognizing all the mentioned parts somewhere in the media is insufficient.

Keep the first implementation small: one to three event clauses, semantic roles such as agent and object, local attributes, conjunction, and scoped negation. Add temporal relations as an extension. Keep the visual and audio models frozen. Train only small relation classifiers and calibration parameters initially. A large new training corpus and multimodal language-model fine-tuning are outside the initial plan.

Use “probabilistic parsing” to describe compiling semantic structure and inferring alternative evidence assignments. We are not claiming a new general-purpose syntactic parser or probabilistic inference engine. Shared-variable logic and differentiable probabilistic inference are established tools; the paper must demonstrate the value of our claim representation, evidence interface, and binding-learning procedure.

2. From a claim to a probability

Figure 1 separates language analysis, perception, relation classification, and inference. This separation lets us replace a component without rewriting the entire verifier.

A dependency parse supplies syntax, not a complete logical form. Semantic role labeling identifies who acts and what is affected; additional rules preserve negation, conjunction, reference, and assertion scope. We retain links from each predicate to the original claim span so omitted or mistranslated content can be audited.

3. Claim compilation and shared variables

For “Alice hugs Dave,” a simplified rule is:

supports(hugs(A, B)) :-
    event(E),
    action(E, hug),
    agent(E, A),
    theme(E, B).

The variable E is shared: the agent and object must belong to the same event. Here theme is the semantic role of the participant receiving or undergoing the action. The words Alice and Dave are schematic names for identifiable participants; real examples may use descriptions such as “the person in red.”

This rule specifies the meaning of the claim, while the predicates can have uncertain, learnable outputs. For the first implementation, a complete predicate–argument tuple can be scored jointly. This avoids treating several overlapping observations from the same model as independent facts.

% Schematic neural-predicate interface, not a complete runnable program.
nn(binding_net, [E, R, A, B], Z,
   [supported, refuted, unknown]) :: binding_status(E, R, A, B, Z).

supports(hugs(A, B)) :-
    event(E),
    binding_status(E, hug, A, B, supported).

binding_net looks up cached features for the specified event and ordered arguments. Event identifiers are lookup keys, not learned features. The example shows the support query only; separate rules compute refutation and insufficient evidence. Negation is implemented through those explicit states, not by treating failure to prove support as refutation.

A decoder-only language model may help translate an ambiguous construction into a constrained logical form. It is a frozen fallback, with syntax checking, span coverage checks, and a restricted output vocabulary. It does not get to omit a difficult part of the claim. Manually checked logical forms are an evaluation diagnostic, not the input assumed by the final system.

Candidate retrieval must not assume the complete claim is true. Retrieve possible actions and participants, retain a few plausible events, and then test their bindings. Otherwise a false claim can disappear before it is verified.

4. Evidence, modality, and candidate assignments

The user selected typed support from visual depiction, non-speech sound, speech, and optical character recognition (OCR). These are evidence routes, not mutually exclusive truth labels. A claim can use more than one route.

Keep “the video shows the event” separate from “the narrator says the event happened.” An assertion supports an assertion proposition; it does not automatically establish the event in the world. Verification is relative to the supplied media and the declared evidence policy.

5. What neural network should parameterize a binding function?

Initial choice: a small classifier over cached evidence features.

Start with logistic regression as the control and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with one hidden layer as the main learned relation classifier. Use a BERT-style encoder only when text representations add useful information. Keep decoder-only models frozen for parsing and media understanding.

The input determines the architecture. Numeric scores need a classifier; text descriptions can benefit from a text encoder; raw video and audio still require their own perception models. A text-only BERT model cannot independently inspect the media.

Let \(x\) be the feature vector for one candidate relation, \(r\) its relation type, and \(z\) its evidence status. The output has three entries: supported, refuted, and unknown. A one-hidden-layer classifier can be written as:

\[p_\theta(z\mid x,r)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(W_2\,\operatorname{ReLU}(W_1[x;e_r]+b_1)+b_2\right)_z\]

Here \(e_r\) is a small learned relation embedding, the bracket means concatenation, and \(W_1,W_2,b_1,b_2\) are trainable weights and biases. Use 64 hidden units initially. As an illustrative size calculation, 48 total input features and three outputs give 3,331 classifier parameters, excluding any relation-embedding table. The actual input dimension will follow the feature specification; this is not a measured model size.

Share one classifier across examples and use relation-type features or embeddings, rather than training a separate network for every event label. Begin with standardized numeric features, weight decay, early stopping on development videos, and a fixed small architecture. If text features are added, freeze their encoder and use strong regularization. More parameters are an experimental choice, not a necessary part of the framework.

Learning an ambiguous evidence assignment

When one claim mention could refer to several observed participants or events, a second small scoring head can choose among those candidates. Let \(g_j\) be candidate assignment \(j\), \(x_j\) its cached features, \(s_\eta\) a scalar scoring function with parameters \(\eta\), and \(K\) the number of retained alternatives, including an unavailable option:

\[\alpha_\eta(g_j)=\frac{\exp s_\eta(x_j)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp s_\eta(x_k)}\]

Start with a linear scorer or a 64-unit MLP using the same kinds of features as the relation classifier. Encode the normalized alternatives as one categorical neural predicate; gradients from the final claim loss can update this scorer too. Repeated references to the same claim variable share that categorical choice. A fixed proposal distribution is the control.

Only competing interpretations of one mention are mutually exclusive. Two genuinely different events can both occur; their existence must not be forced into one categorical choice. Keep the program's existential event rules separate from ambiguity about which observed instance a mention refers to. Candidate coverage and identifiability remain empirical limitations.

Information limit: if a correct claim and a swapped claim produce identical classifier inputs, no classifier architecture can reliably separate them from those inputs. The first audit must check whether full-tuple scores or candidate-specific observations actually contain binding information.

6. Fixed functions, learned functions, and inference

A predefined composition rule is a conditional distribution with all its mass on one output. Let \(f_r\) be the rule for relation/operator \(r\), \(\mathbf z\) the child evidence states, \(\mathbf g\) the candidate assignments, and \(z_p\) the parent state:

\[Q_r^{\mathrm{rule}}(z_p\mid\mathbf z,\mathbf g)=\mathbf 1[z_p=f_r(\mathbf z,\mathbf g)]\]

The indicator is one when the output matches the rule and zero otherwise. A learned \(Q_r\) has the same inputs and a normalized output distribution. A nonnegative unnormalized function is a factor potential instead; its normalization must be specified. “Interchangeable” means that the domains and meanings match, not that every replacement preserves accuracy or calibration.

Keep logical operators deterministic in the first implementation, while learning the media-relation predicates. For conjunction: all required parts supported gives supported; an explicitly refuted required part gives refuted; otherwise the result is unknown. Negation exchanges supported and refuted while preserving unknown. Learned composition distributions can be compared later under the same interface. They need enough observations to distinguish composition errors from perception errors.

DeepProbLog-style inference computes a query probability by summing the probabilities of possible worlds that satisfy it. Let \(q\) be a query, \(E\) the media observations, \(\theta\) the learned parameters, and \(\omega\) an assignment of the modeled uncertain variables:

\[P_\theta(q\mid E)=\sum_{\omega:\,\omega\models q}P_\theta(\omega\mid E)\]

This is not arbitrary multiplication of every probe score. Repeated uses of a canonical fact refer to the same random variable. Distinct probabilistic facts are independent by default unless dependence is explicitly modeled. To reduce an obvious source of error, feed overlapping primitive scores into one joint relation classifier instead of also multiplying them as independent evidence.

Mutually exclusive alternatives can be represented by an annotated disjunction: one categorical choice with probabilities for supported, refuted, and unknown. The compiler must ensure that the root-state queries partition the modeled outcomes. Unknown is an explicit category; it is not the logical negation of supported. ProbLog's model documentation explains probabilistic facts and categorical choices.

One supporting event can support an existential claim. Failure to retrieve such an event does not establish refutation when candidate coverage is incomplete. Refutation requires explicit contrary evidence or a defensible coverage condition. Multiple overlapping windows must not be treated as independent confirmations of the same event.

Use a bounded candidate set and a restricted claim language first. Exact inference may become expensive as alternatives and shared variables grow. A dependency tree does not guarantee that the resulting probabilistic program has tree structure. Benchmark query grounding, compilation, inference time, and memory; use existing inference before considering a custom implementation.

8. Learning from claim labels with frozen media models

Compiling fixed scores into a program performs inference only. To learn, make the relation probabilities or selected composition parameters trainable and optimize the final claim predictions. DeepProbLog provides differentiable probabilistic inference; we can reuse this capability.

  1. Run the frozen media models once and cache their outputs for each candidate relation.
  2. Use the small classifier to produce local evidence-state distributions.
  3. Run inference to obtain the root distribution for the compiled claim.
  4. Compute the loss against verified claim labels and update the small classifier through inference.

Let \(N\) be the number of training claims, \(c_i\) claim \(i\), \(E_i\) its media evidence, and \(y_i\) its supported/refuted/unknown label. Let \(z_{\mathrm{root}}\) be the inferred root state. With optional local relation labels \(\mathcal A\), use:

\[\begin{aligned}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{claim}}&=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log P_\theta(z_{\mathrm{root}}=y_i\mid c_i,E_i),\\\mathcal L&=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{claim}}+\lambda\mathcal L_{\mathrm{local}},\quad\lambda\geq 0.\end{aligned}\]

\(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{local}}\) is cross-entropy on the small set \(\mathcal A\) of annotated local relations, and \(\lambda\) controls its weight. Set \(\lambda=0\) when only claim labels are available. Weight decay is applied by the optimizer. All media-model parameters stay frozen; gradients update the relation classifier and any explicitly selected probabilistic parameters.

Final claim labels do not uniquely determine every intermediate relation. Validated role swaps and event reassignments help because the parts remain present while the binding changes. A small amount of local relation supervision can help further. Binary support labels alone cannot fully identify the distinction between refuted and unknown; include explicit unknown examples and availability rules.

Separate three training targets: relation prediction learns who participates in an event; composition/calibration learns how local evidence relates to final support; structure learning discovers program structure. The first two are in scope. Unrestricted discovery of arbitrary rules is not part of the initial plan.

9. Temporal constraints as an extension

Attach original intervals to event candidates and add fixed or learned temporal compatibility functions. This changes the available assignments, not the overall verification task.

The proposed union of parent and child intervals is a useful candidate-window baseline. It is not evidence that they form one event: a set union can retain disconnected events, and its enclosing interval can span a long unrelated section. For local agent/object bindings, compare shared-event candidates with full-tuple verification. For before/after, keep ordered intervals that may be disjoint. For audio/visual attachment, retain synchronization tolerance and source identity.

Compare full clip, parent/child union, oracle event window, and automatic candidate windows. Give whole-claim verification the same selected windows. Count localization calls and control frame density; a crop should not receive uncounted extra visual information. The FV-Action preprint is a relevant frozen temporal-grounding baseline.

10. Small, validated training and test sets

Use real media with synthetic claim edits. We do not need to generate a large synthetic video corpus. The original statement and the edited statement both need evidence-based checking; grammatical edits do not guarantee a changed truth value.

11. Experiments that establish the method's value

The primary research question is whether the compiled representation and learned binding probabilities improve verification, generalization, or evidence use under a controlled budget. No particular inference engine or neural architecture is presumed to win.

Claim verification and hallucination detection share the evidence-support operation. Start with a small prompt-development batch

  1. Choose 10 seed claims with checked evidence and explicit participant/event references. If a seed is only model-proposed, mark that and review it before treating its variants as labeled examples.
  2. Generate at most three variants per seed: an eligible role/event/attribute edit, a supported paraphrase, and optionally a second eligible binding edit. Returning no candidate is allowed.
  3. Run deterministic checks for valid records, duplicates, one intended semantic change, and permitted vocabulary. Then review claims individually against media, without revealing their proposed labels or partners.
  4. I inspect accepted/rejected examples and disagreements, examine accessible evidence and logs, revise prompts on development data, and rerun the affected records. If I have only a model description, my review does not independently certify the underlying event.

Only the intended claim/evidence features enter the classifier; construction metadata and the gold partner do not.

Training labels and test labels have different requirements

Training: automatically labeled examples can provide inexpensive supervision. These are often called silver labels: labels not fully checked by a person. Audit a random sample stratified by edit type, modality, label, and reviewer agreement; inspect difficult disagreements too. Report automatic versus independently reviewed counts separately.

Evaluation: keep a small independently checked test set. Otherwise a system can score well by reproducing the model that supplied its labels. Freeze prompts and thresholds before the final test; do not retain only examples on which a baseline already agrees with the proposed answer. A second model can reduce some shared errors, but agreement between models is not a correctness guarantee.

Minimal text edits can still introduce language-only clues. Include positive paraphrases, fluent negatives, and a text-only baseline. SugarCrepe uses language models to construct fluent negatives and addresses benchmark shortcuts. Self-Instruct establishes a generation/filtering approach for instruction data; it does not establish that generated multimodal claims are true. LLM-as-a-judge research documents judge biases. Our media-specific review and independent-label policy are design recommendations, not results inherited from those papers.

16. Implementation order and remaining decisions

  1. First three days: specify the restricted logical forms, observation schema, candidate identifiers, and 40–60-pair pilot. Verify whether cached observations contain binding signal.
  2. Rest of week one: compile a small set into an existing probabilistic backend; implement explicit supported/refuted/unknown rules; run direct, fixed-rule, and linear-classifier controls.
  3. Week two: train the small relation classifier through claim-level inference; compare alternative candidate assignments and one module replacement. Add frozen text features only if the numeric input audit gives a reason.
  4. Week three: evaluate sealed source-video splits, unknown/paraphrase controls, a genuinely audiovisual subset, and temporal constraints. Draft around observed behavior and limitations.

Plan roughly 15–35 annotation hours for the scoped pilot/test and 20–100 GPU-hours for frozen-model inference, subject to measured latency and query count. Classifier training should be much cheaper than media inference. These are estimates, not allocated jobs or measured costs. Reuse existing media and cache observations rather than launching a large data-generation campaign.

Decisions still needed during implementation
  • Which candidate observations can be matched to stable event/participant identities without building a new tracking system?
  • Which numeric features distinguish the correct tuple from its role swap?
  • Do explicit unknown labels and candidate-coverage rules provide enough supervision for refuted versus unavailable evidence?
  • Does a text encoder improve relation prediction after controlling media observations and training labels?
  • Which fixed-rule and learned-function configurations can be recovered exactly under the same program semantics?
  • How much calibration must be refitted after a perception module is replaced?

Terminology: use familiar terms such as semantic parsing, logical form, semantic role, grounding, relation classifier, neural predicate, conditional distribution, probabilistic inference, and calibration. Define them when first used. Do not invent names for ordinary components or use a new acronym to make the method sound novel.
